\begin{abstract}
Online Gaussian processes (GPs) are attractive for streaming prediction because they combine flexible function modeling with calibrated uncertainty, but exact inference is cubic and standard sparse online updates can forget early observations. Online HiPPO Sparse Variational Gaussian Processes (OHSVGP) addresses this issue by using HiPPO-based interdomain inducing variables, yet its interdomain kernel construction is built from temporal ODE recursion of Fourier--Legendre states. We study an analytic replacement for this kernel-construction step in the one-dimensional HiPPO-LegS setting with stationary kernels. The main idea is to express the required sine/cosine--Legendre overlap integrals in closed form through spherical Bessel functions, so that kernel quantities are evaluated directly at time $t$ and the remaining recurrence is over polynomial order rather than over timestamps. This changes the recursion structure of interdomain kernel construction, removes ODE discretization error from that construction, and reduces its asymptotic dependence on the number of ODE integration steps needed to reach the current horizon. On Solar Irradiance and COVID-19 mortality streams, the resulting analytic variant maintains comparable predictive performance and is slightly favorable on several aggregate metrics relative to ODE-based OHSVGP. The present derivation is specific to one-dimensional HiPPO-LegS with stationary kernels, and runtime scaling on the solar benchmark confirms the predicted reduction in discretization-step dependence, with the strongest gains at fine time discretizations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Gaussian processes (GPs) remain a standard probabilistic tool for time-series modeling since they combine nonparametric flexibility with principled uncertainty quantification \citep{Roberts2013}. Their deployment in online settings, however, is limited by the cubic complexity of exact inference and by the fact that sparse online approximations \citep{Titsias2009,Hensman2013,Bui2017,Maddox2021} often struggle with catastrophic forgetting.

The Online HiPPO Sparse Variational Gaussian Process (OHSVGP) framework \citep{Chen2025} addresses this issue by constructing the inducing variables for GPs with high-order polynomial projection operators (HiPPO) \citep{Gu2020}, substantially alleviating catastrophic forgetting in online regression and continual-learning settings. By defining inducing features as HiPPO's time-varying orthogonal polynomials over $[0,t]$, a fixed set of inducing variables optimally compresses the entire observation history as the horizon grows.

Despite these benefits, the original implementation relies on ODE-based evolution of the interdomain features, which requires discretization to integrate over time. Therefore, as finer ODE discretizations are used to reach the current horizon, integration becomes computationally expensive and discretization errors accumulate. We observe that for HiPPO-LegS in one dimension, the sine/cosine--Legendre inner products required by the interdomain construction admit closed forms via spherical Bessel functions, removing the need for ODE recursion altogether.
%なので、時系列が長くなると積分にコストが掛かったり、あるいは誤差の蓄積とかが問題になったりする


Our contributions are threefold. First, we show that the sine/cosine--Legendre overlaps underlying the OHSVGP interdomain random-feature matrix admit closed forms via spherical Bessel functions, yielding a direct analytic construction of $Z(t)$ and hence the same RFF approximations to $K_{fu}(t)$ and $K_{uu}(t)$ without temporal ODE evolution. Second, we demonstrate that the analytic construction avoids per-step ODE recursion, reducing kernel cost significantly when the number of ODE integration steps far exceeds the basis size, and eliminates the discretization error that accumulates over long horizons. Third, we validate on Solar Irradiance and COVID-19 mortality streams that the analytic variant preserves predictive behavior comparable to ODE-based OHSVGP.

\section{Background}

Here we review the necessary background on GPs and their sparse approximations, and interdomain GPs, then introduce the online HiPPO sparse GP framework which motivates our analytic extension.

\subsection{Gaussian Processes, Sparse Variational Inference, and Online Updates}

Let $f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0,k)$ be a Gaussian process with covariance kernel $k$ \citep{Rasmussen2006}. Given data $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, exact GP inference scales cubically in $N$, motivating sparse variational approximations based on inducing variables $u$ \citep{Titsias2009,Hensman2013}. In the standard formulation, the variational posterior is defined as
\begin{equation}
q(f,u)=p(f \mid u)q(u),
\end{equation}
where $q(u)$ is usually Gaussian with free mean and covariance. For Gaussian likelihoods, collapsed objectives are available; for non-conjugate likelihoods, stochastic variational inference is typically used \citep{Hensman2015}.

Interdomain GPs generalize inducing variables from point evaluations to integral transforms,
\begin{equation}
u_m = \int f(x)\phi_m(x)\,dx,
\end{equation}
where $\{\phi_m\}$ are basis functions chosen to induce desirable structure \citep{LazaroGredilla2009,VanderWilk2020}. The corresponding covariances are
\begin{equation}
[K_{fu}]_m = \int k(x,x')\phi_m(x')\,dx',
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[K_{uu}]_{nm} = \iint k(x,x')\phi_n(x)\phi_m(x')\,dx\,dx'.
\end{equation}

In online learning, data arrive sequentially. Standard online sparse GP and online sparse variational GP methods update the posterior using only new data and the previous variational approximation \citep{Csato2002,Bui2017,Maddox2021}. While computationally attractive, this approach does not guarantee memory retention, as inducing locations remain optimized relative to recent observations.

At update step $t$, such methods optimize a variational objective on the newly arrived batch while regularizing the new variational posterior toward the information carried forward from the previous step:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_t(q_t)
=
\mathbb{E}_{q_t(f)}\!\left[\log p(y_t \mid f)\right]
-
\mathrm{KL}\!\left[q_t(u)\,\|\,\widetilde{p}_{t-1}(u)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde{p}_{t-1}(u)$ denotes the predictive prior or transferred variational information from the previous update. In this paper, the online variational objective is unchanged from OHSVGP; the `one ELBO update step' runtime in Section~\ref{sec:runtime-scaling} refers to evaluating this objective, backpropagating through it, and taking one optimizer step.

\subsection{HiPPO, Recurrent Memory, and OHSVGP}

HiPPO constructs a finite-dimensional memory state by projecting historical signals onto time-varying orthogonal polynomial bases \citep{Gu2020}. For HiPPO-LegS, the basis functions are scaled Legendre polynomials over the interval $[0,t]$, producing coefficients that satisfy a linear ODE:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}c(t) = A(t)c(t) + B(t)f(t),
\end{equation}
supporting streaming updates and long-range memory.

Building on this memory construction, OHSVGP turns HiPPO coefficients into interdomain inducing variables \citep{Chen2025},
\begin{equation}
u_m(t)=\int f(x)\phi_m^{(t)}(x)\,dx,
\end{equation}
with time-dependent basis $\phi_m^{(t)}$. In the one-dimensional HiPPO-LegS case considered here, the basis functions on $[0,t]$ are
\begin{equation}
\phi_\ell^{(t)}(x) = \frac{2\ell+1}{t}P_\ell\!\left(\frac{2x}{t}-1\right)\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(x),
\end{equation}
We use this normalization throughout the paper; alternative orthonormal Legendre conventions differ only by row-wise scaling factors in the feature matrix.
These basis functions induce the interdomain quantities
\begin{equation}
[K_{fu}(t)]_{n\ell} = \int_0^t k(x_n,x)\phi_\ell^{(t)}(x)\,dx,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
[K_{uu}(t)]_{\ell m} = \int_0^t\int_0^t k(x,x')\phi_\ell^{(t)}(x)\phi_m^{(t)}(x')\,dx\,dx'.
\end{equation}
In the original OHSVGP implementation, these kernel terms are built in Random Fourier Feature (RFF) space~\citep{Rahimi2007,Hensman2018} by evolving Fourier-Legendre coefficients recursively in $t$ with a HiPPO ODE, so that kernel construction is tied to a time-stepping procedure whose cost and discretization error both grow with the number of ODE integration steps.

\subsection{Spectral Factorization of the Interdomain Kernels}
\label{sec:bochner}

Starting from the HiPPO-LegS basis and interdomain kernels introduced above, we next recall the spectral factorization that underlies the analytic construction. We assume throughout that the kernel $k$ is stationary and admits a spectral density $p(\omega)$, so that Bochner's theorem gives \citep{Rudin1994,Rahimi2007}
\begin{align}
k(x,x') = \mathbb{E}_{\omega \sim p(\omega)}\bigl[&\cos(\omega x)\cos(\omega x') \notag \\
&+ \sin(\omega x)\sin(\omega x')\bigr].
\end{align}
Drawing $R$ independent frequencies $\{\omega_r\}_{r=1}^{R} \sim p(\omega)$ replaces the expectation by an empirical average. Substituting this factorization into $K_{fu}(t)$ and $K_{uu}(t)$ and exchanging expectation with integration over $x$ shows that each sampled frequency $\omega$ contributes to the kernels only through a pair of one-dimensional projections of the HiPPO-LegS basis:
\begin{equation}
C_\ell(t;\omega)=\int_0^t \cos(\omega x)\,\phi_\ell^{(t)}(x)\,dx,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
S_\ell(t;\omega)=\int_0^t \sin(\omega x)\,\phi_\ell^{(t)}(x)\,dx.
\end{equation}
Stacking the cosine and sine projections over all sampled
frequencies gives the interdomain random-feature matrix
$Z(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 2R}$. For column index
$q=1,\ldots,2R$, define
\begin{equation}
Z(t)_{\ell,q}
=
R^{-1/2}
\begin{cases}
C_\ell(t;\omega_q), & 1\le q\le R,\\
S_\ell(t;\omega_{q-R}), & R< q\le 2R.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The corresponding data-side RFF matrix
$Z_f(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2R}$ is
\begin{equation}
Z_f(X)_{n,q}
=
R^{-1/2}
\begin{cases}
\cos(\omega_q x_n), & 1\le q\le R,\\
\sin(\omega_{q-R}x_n), & R< q\le 2R.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The RFF approximation then assembles the interdomain
covariance matrices as
\begin{equation}
K_{uu}(t) \approx Z(t)Z(t)^\top,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
K_{fu}(t) \approx Z_f(X)Z(t)^\top.
\end{equation}
Thus, after the Bochner/RFF reduction, the computational bottleneck is not the matrix product itself but the construction of $Z(t)$, i.e., the evaluation of the $2MR$ sine/cosine--Legendre overlaps at the current horizon $t$. The original OHSVGP construction obtains these overlaps by evolving Fourier-feature inputs through the HiPPO-LegS ODE in time~\citep{Chen2025}, whereas our contribution is to compute the same overlaps directly in closed form.

\section{Methods}

We now turn to the new part of the construction. In the one-dimensional HiPPO-LegS setting with a stationary kernel, the overlap integrals identified in Section~\ref{sec:bochner} can be evaluated at a target time $t$ in closed form, without the temporal HiPPO ODE recursion that the baseline OHSVGP uses to construct them. The derivation uses the plane-wave/Legendre identity to express every entry of the RFF feature matrix $Z(t)$ underlying $K_{fu}(t)$ and $K_{uu}(t)$ in terms of spherical Bessel functions, after which the sequence $\{j_\ell\}$ is generated by a short recursion over polynomial order rather than over time.

We therefore present the method in two steps. Section~\ref{sec:closed-form} derives the closed-form expression of each overlap via the plane-wave/Legendre identity. Section~\ref{sec:numerical} describes the polynomial-order recursion used to evaluate the resulting spherical Bessel sequence and summarizes the computational cost. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} contrasts the full construction with the baseline ODE-based assembly.

\subsection{Closed-Form via Spherical Bessel Functions}
\label{sec:closed-form}

The key observation is that a change of variables to $u = 2x/t - 1$ turns $C_\ell$ and $S_\ell$ into the classical plane-wave/Legendre integral, which is known in closed form via spherical Bessel functions. The choice of $u$ matches the argument of the Legendre polynomial in $\phi_\ell^{(t)}$, which already has the form $P_\ell(2x/t - 1)$, and maps $[0,t]$ to the standard Legendre domain $[-1,1]$.

We combine $C_\ell$ and $S_\ell$ into a single complex integral
\begin{equation}
C_\ell(t;\omega) + i\,S_\ell(t;\omega) = \int_0^t e^{i\omega x}\,\phi_\ell^{(t)}(x)\,dx,
\end{equation}
and introduce
\begin{equation}
u = \frac{2x}{t}-1,\qquad \kappa = \frac{\omega t}{2}.
\end{equation}
After substitution, the Legendre polynomial depends on $u$, and the exponential becomes a plane wave $e^{i\omega x} = e^{i\kappa(u+1)} = e^{i\kappa}\,e^{i\kappa u}$. Applying the classical plane-wave/Legendre identity~\citep{DLMF}
\begin{equation}
\int_{-1}^1 e^{i\kappa u}\,P_\ell(u)\,du = 2\,i^\ell\,j_\ell(\kappa),
\end{equation}
where $j_\ell$ is the spherical Bessel function of the first kind, closes the remaining integral. Factoring out $e^{i\kappa}$ and using $i^\ell = \cos(\ell\pi/2) + i\sin(\ell\pi/2)$, the real and imaginary parts give the closed-form overlaps
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:C-closed}
C_\ell(t;\omega) = (2\ell+1)\,j_\ell(\kappa)\,\cos(\kappa+\ell\pi/2),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:S-closed}
S_\ell(t;\omega) = (2\ell+1)\,j_\ell(\kappa)\,\sin(\kappa+\ell\pi/2),
\end{equation}
with $\kappa = \omega t/2$.

In these closed forms, $t$ enters only through the scalar $\kappa = \omega t/2$; the polynomial order $\ell$ enters through the Bessel index and the phase shift. Using the RFF assembly introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bochner}, these closed-form overlaps define the same interdomain feature matrix $Z(t)$ used by the ODE-based OHSVGP construction. The difference is therefore not the variational objective or the RFF kernel factorization, but the way $Z(t)$ is obtained: directly at the target horizon $t$, rather than by advancing a HiPPO-LegS ODE over all preceding discretization steps.

\subsection{Numerical Evaluation and Complexity}
\label{sec:numerical}

The only remaining computational task is the evaluation of $\{j_\ell(\kappa)\}_{\ell=0}^{M-1}$ for each sampled frequency. The three-term recurrence
\begin{equation}
j_{\ell-1}(\kappa) = \frac{2\ell+1}{\kappa}\,j_\ell(\kappa) - j_{\ell+1}(\kappa)
\end{equation}
is numerically unstable when applied upward from $j_0, j_1$ once $\ell$ exceeds $\kappa$, because it amplifies the irregular Bessel solution. We therefore evaluate it by backward Miller recursion~\citep{Gautschi1967}: starting from a sufficiently large order $L \gg M$ with an arbitrary non-zero seed pair $(j_L, j_{L+1})$, we apply the recurrence downward to $\ell = 0$ and rescale the whole sequence so that $j_0(\kappa) = \sin(\kappa)/\kappa$ matches its known value. For small $\kappa$, Taylor expansions around the origin avoid division-by-zero in this normalization. The procedure is purely arithmetic and compatible with auto-differentiation, and it yields all $M$ orders in $\mathcal{O}(M)$ work per frequency.

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-check} reports a representative convergence check for the time-stepped ODE construction against the analytic feature evaluation. We measure
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RelErr}(\Delta t)
=
\frac{\|Z_{\mathrm{ODE},\Delta t}(t)-Z_{\mathrm{analytic}}(t)\|_F}
{\|Z_{\mathrm{analytic}}(t)\|_F}.
\end{equation}
The relative feature error decreases as the ODE step size $\Delta t$ becomes smaller and follows an approximately first-order trend over the tested range. This diagnostic is intended to verify the ODE feature discretization rather than to compare predictive performance on a particular dataset. It supports the interpretation that the analytic construction removes ODE time-stepping error from feature evaluation itself, while the remaining approximation error is due to the finite spectral representation rather than temporal discretization.

For a stream discretized into $T$ ODE integration steps, the ODE-based construction need not store the full trajectory: it maintains only the current Fourier--Legendre projection state for each sampled frequency, requiring $\mathcal{O}(RM)$ feature-state memory and $\mathcal{O}(M^2)$ additional storage for $K_{uu}(t)$. Its time cost, however, remains sequential in the number of integration steps. Computing $Z(t)$ at the final horizon requires integrating the HiPPO-LegS feature ODE over $[0,t]$, equivalently applying $T$ recurrent updates; with dense $M$-dimensional transitions, this costs $\mathcal{O}(RM^2T)$. In contrast, the analytic construction evaluates $Z(t)$ directly at the target horizon. The Bessel-based projections cost $\mathcal{O}(RM)$, while the dominant inducing-kernel assembly $K_{uu}(t)\approx Z(t)Z(t)^\top$ costs $\mathcal{O}(RM^2)$; constructing $K_{fu}(t)\approx Z_f(X)Z(t)^\top$ costs $\mathcal{O}(NRM)$ for $N$ data inputs. Thus, the analytic construction removes the linear dependence on the number of preceding ODE integration steps while retaining the same leading storage order. Section~\ref{sec:runtime-scaling} verifies this reduced discretization-step dependence in wall-clock measurements and also shows that the current pure-torch Miller implementation has substantial overhead in the moderate-discretization basis-size sweep, despite favorable FLOP estimates.

The two variants share the same data split, kernel family, initialization, inducing budget, and spectral budget. We report task-wise RMSE and predictive negative log-likelihood (NLL), and defer representative posterior plots to Appendix~\ref{app:solar-results}.

\subsection{Efficiency and Scaling Evaluation}
\label{sec:runtime-scaling}

We next test whether the change in recursion structure yields practical runtime gains on the solar benchmark while preserving predictive accuracy. Both methods use the same kernel family, spectral budget ($R=1000$), dtype, device, and random seed. All timing experiments were run on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 in double precision. We report wall-clock time for interdomain kernel construction as medians over 20 repeats after 5 warmup iterations, and we report test RMSE after fitting the variational parameters under fixed kernel hyperparameters. To complement wall-clock time, we additionally report profiler-estimated FLOP counts from PyTorch's \texttt{with\_flops=True} profiler. These FLOP numbers are operator-level estimates rather than hardware peak throughput; for memory robustness they were collected with a reduced profiling budget $R=512$ while keeping the same discretization-count and $M$ sweeps. Appendix~\ref{app:runtime-results} reports the corresponding summary tables.

In the runtime sweeps, $T$ denotes the number of HiPPO/ODE discretization steps rather than the number of solar observations. In these benchmarks, $T$ is \emph{not} the number of solar observations. The solar dataset contains only a few hundred samples, whereas the sweep uses $T \in \{100,300,1000,3000,7000\}$ as the number of HiPPO/ODE discretization steps over a fixed time interval. The more faithful horizontal axis is therefore the integration step size
\[
\Delta t = \frac{t_{\max} - t_{\min}}{T},
\]
so increasing $T$ corresponds to a finer temporal discretization rather than to a longer dataset. We therefore plot the runtime and RMSE curves against $\Delta t$, with the corresponding $T$ values shown in parentheses on the tick labels.

Figure~\ref{fig:runtime-vs-t} isolates the kernel-construction cost against $\Delta t$ and compares the resulting test RMSE on the same sweep. The left panel is the direct runtime test of the theoretical claim in Section~\ref{sec:numerical}: as $\Delta t$ decreases, the ODE baseline must traverse more temporal steps, whereas the analytic construction avoids that temporal recursion and therefore removes the dominant linear dependence on the discretization count. Quantitatively, the ODE kernel time rises from $5.1$\,ms at $T=100$ to $282.9$\,ms at $T=7000$, while the analytic kernel stays in a much flatter $37.8$--$76.8$\,ms band and is $7.5\times$ faster at the finest discretization. The profiler-estimated kernel FLOP count shows the same trend more cleanly: the ODE path grows from $1.02$\,GFLOPs to $59.37$\,GFLOPs, whereas the analytic path remains essentially constant at $\approx 0.177$\,GFLOPs. The right panel checks the downstream predictive side of the same ablation. Here both methods remain in a narrow and overlapping RMSE band on solar (ODE: $0.155$--$0.163$, analytic: $0.157$--$0.162$), indicating that refining $\Delta t$ mainly changes computational cost rather than predictive accuracy under the fixed-kernel evaluation used here.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We presented an analytic replacement for ODE-based interdomain kernel construction in one-dimensional HiPPO-LegS OHSVGP. The central change is a shift from temporal ODE recursion to direct kernel evaluation together with recursion over polynomial order, implemented through spherical Bessel identities. Within this restricted setting, the resulting construction removes ODE discretization error from interdomain kernel construction and reduces its asymptotic dependence on the number of ODE integration steps needed to reach the current horizon, while maintaining comparable predictive performance on the evaluated benchmarks. The runtime study confirms that the expected reduction in discretization-step dependence appears in practice at fine $\Delta t$, while also showing that the current pure-torch Miller implementation is slower in wall-clock time in the moderate-discretization basis-size sweep despite favorable FLOP estimates. Important next steps are to study alternative basis functions and spectral approximations, to broaden the runtime evaluation across hardware and spectral budgets, and to determine whether analogous analytic constructions are possible in higher-dimensional settings.

\section{Closed-form derivation of the analytic overlaps}
\label{app:closed-form}

We derive the closed-form expressions used in Section~\ref{sec:closed-form}. For the HiPPO-LegS inducing feature convention used in this paper,
\[
\phi_\ell^{(t)}(x)
=
\frac{2\ell+1}{t}
P_\ell\!\left(\frac{2x}{t}-1\right)
\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(x),
\]
the complex sine/cosine overlap is
\[
C_\ell(t;\omega)+iS_\ell(t;\omega)
=
\int_0^t e^{i\omega x}\phi_\ell^{(t)}(x)\,dx.
\]
Let
\[
u=\frac{2x}{t}-1,
\qquad
x=\frac{t}{2}(u+1),
\qquad
dx=\frac{t}{2}\,du,
\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\omega t}{2}.
\]
Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
C_\ell(t;\omega)+iS_\ell(t;\omega)
&=
\frac{2\ell+1}{2}
e^{i\kappa}
\int_{-1}^{1}
e^{i\kappa u}P_\ell(u)\,du .
\end{aligned}
\]
Using the plane-wave/Legendre identity~\citep{DLMF},
\[
\int_{-1}^{1} e^{i\kappa u}P_\ell(u)\,du
=
2i^\ell j_\ell(\kappa),
\]
where \(j_\ell\) is the spherical Bessel function of the first kind, gives
\[
C_\ell(t;\omega)+iS_\ell(t;\omega)
=
(2\ell+1)j_\ell(\kappa)e^{i(\kappa+\ell\pi/2)}.
\]
Taking real and imaginary parts yields
\[
C_\ell(t;\omega)
=
(2\ell+1)j_\ell(\kappa)\cos(\kappa+\ell\pi/2),
\]
and
\[
S_\ell(t;\omega)
=
(2\ell+1)j_\ell(\kappa)\sin(\kappa+\ell\pi/2),
\]
which are the closed-form overlaps used to construct the analytic feature matrix \(Z(t)\).

\section{RFF assembly and normalization}
\label{app:rff-normalization}

We spell out the normalization used in the RFF interdomain kernel assembly. For each sampled frequency \(\omega_r\), define the vectors
\[
C(t;\omega_r)
=
\bigl(C_0(t;\omega_r),\ldots,C_{M-1}(t;\omega_r)\bigr)^\top,
\]
and
\[
S(t;\omega_r)
=
\bigl(S_0(t;\omega_r),\ldots,S_{M-1}(t;\omega_r)\bigr)^\top.
\]
The interdomain feature matrix can be written compactly as
\[
Z(t)
=
R^{-1/2}
\bigl[
C(t;\omega_1),\ldots,C(t;\omega_R),
S(t;\omega_1),\ldots,S(t;\omega_R)
\bigr]
\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 2R}.
\]
Similarly, for data inputs \(X=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\), define
\[
c_r(X)
=
\bigl(\cos(\omega_r x_1),\ldots,\cos(\omega_r x_N)\bigr)^\top,
\]
and
\[
s_r(X)
=
\bigl(\sin(\omega_r x_1),\ldots,\sin(\omega_r x_N)\bigr)^\top,
\]
so that
\[
Z_f(X)
=
R^{-1/2}
\bigl[
c_1(X),\ldots,c_R(X),
s_1(X),\ldots,s_R(X)
\bigr]
\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2R}.
\]

With this convention,
\[
Z(t)Z(t)^\top
=
\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}
\left[
C(t;\omega_r)C(t;\omega_r)^\top
+
S(t;\omega_r)S(t;\omega_r)^\top
\right].
\]
Elementwise, this gives
\[
[Z(t)Z(t)^\top]_{\ell m}
=
\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}
\left[
C_\ell(t;\omega_r)C_m(t;\omega_r)
+
S_\ell(t;\omega_r)S_m(t;\omega_r)
\right],
\]
which is the Monte Carlo approximation to the Bochner-factorized inducing covariance \(K_{uu}(t)\) for stationary kernels~\citep{Rudin1994,Rahimi2007}. Likewise,
\[
[Z_f(X)Z(t)^\top]_{n\ell}
=
\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}
\left[
\cos(\omega_r x_n)C_\ell(t;\omega_r)
+
\sin(\omega_r x_n)S_\ell(t;\omega_r)
\right],
\]
which gives the corresponding RFF approximation to \(K_{fu}(t)\). Therefore the analytic construction changes only the evaluation of the overlap vectors \(C(t;\omega_r)\) and \(S(t;\omega_r)\); the RFF kernel assembly remains the same as in the OHSVGP construction~\citep{Chen2025}.

\section{Numerical details of backward Miller recursion}
\label{app:miller}

The analytic construction requires evaluating
\(\{j_\ell(\kappa)\}_{\ell=0}^{M-1}\) for each sampled frequency. Direct upward recurrence can be unstable when the order exceeds the argument, because the recurrence may amplify the irregular solution. We therefore use backward Miller recursion~\citep{Gautschi1967,DLMF}.

For a target maximum order \(M-1\), choose a truncation order \(L\ge M\) with a safety margin. Starting from an arbitrary nonzero seed pair
\[
\tilde j_L=1,
\qquad
\tilde j_{L+1}=0,
\]
we run the three-term recurrence backward,
\[
\tilde j_{\ell-1}(\kappa)
=
\frac{2\ell+1}{\kappa}\tilde j_{\ell}(\kappa)
-
\tilde j_{\ell+1}(\kappa),
\qquad
\ell=L,L-1,\ldots,1.
\]
The resulting sequence is correct only up to a common scale. We fix this scale using the known value
\[
j_0(\kappa)=\frac{\sin \kappa}{\kappa},
\]
and set
\[
j_\ell(\kappa)
=
\tilde j_\ell(\kappa)
\frac{\sin \kappa}{\kappa\,\tilde j_0(\kappa)},
\qquad
\ell=0,\ldots,M-1.
\]
For \(|\kappa|\) near zero, the normalization above is replaced by the small-argument expansion
\[
j_\ell(\kappa)
=
\frac{\kappa^\ell}{(2\ell+1)!!}
+
\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{\ell+2}),
\]
with the limiting value \(j_0(0)=1\). This avoids division by zero and stabilizes the low-frequency case. The backward pass requires \(\mathcal{O}(L)\) arithmetic operations per frequency, and with a fixed safety margin \(L=M+\mathcal{O}(1)\), this is \(\mathcal{O}(M)\) per frequency.
